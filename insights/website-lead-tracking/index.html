BRICAL FIELD NOTES / PRACTICAL GUIDE

A click is not
a customer.

Separate traffic, inquiries, qualified leads, and booked jobs with a practical measurement plan. Learn what the records can—and cannot—tell you.

IN THIS GUIDE

Define the stages Capture useful records Connect the outcome Interpret carefully

By Brical Group · September 14, 2026

Start by defining the stages.

A website report can look healthy while the business still has unanswered inquiries. The first improvement is not necessarily another tracking tool. It is a shared definition of the steps between a visit and a paid job.

Use a simple sequence that reflects the actual operation:

  1. Visit: someone loaded a page. That does not tell you whether the person is a potential customer.
  2. Inquiry: a person actually contacted the business. A click on a phone link is not proof that a conversation happened.
  3. Qualified lead: the inquiry meets criteria you have defined, such as service type, area, and a genuine need.
  4. Booked job: a job or appointment was scheduled. It may still cancel or change.
  5. Completed and paid: the work happened and payment was recorded. That still is not the same thing as profit.

Not every business uses this exact sequence. The important part is to define yours and use it consistently across the website, customer records, and report.

Capture a small set of useful fields.

A starting lead register can include an inquiry ID, date, recorded source, service requested, current stage, owner, and next action. Keep contact details in the appropriate access-controlled system rather than copying them into every report.

For source information, keep an “unknown” category. A forced guess makes the chart look complete but reduces the quality of the underlying information.

A sensible first check: send a test inquiry through the real contact path and confirm where it arrives. Seeing an on-screen message does not prove that the business received it.

Connect the inquiry to the outcome.

Use a stable identifier to connect the inquiry record with its later status. Decide who updates it and when. Otherwise, your report may call a job “unbooked” simply because nobody entered the outcome.

Calculate rates with an explicit denominator. If five of ten referral inquiries were booked, the inquiry-to-booking rate is 50%. A qualified-lead-to-booking rate answers a different question and will often have a different denominator.

Also be clear about the time period. Bookings in a calendar month may come from inquiries received in an earlier month. A cohort report follows the same group of inquiries over time; an activity report counts what occurred within a period. Both can be useful, but they are not interchangeable.

Do not mistake attribution for proof.

A recorded source is an observation about how the record was labeled. It does not capture every interaction that influenced a customer. A person might discover the business through a referral, read its social profile, and then arrive through search.

Before cutting a channel or increasing its budget, review lead quality, follow-up, capacity, costs, and completed-job outcomes. A few bookings can move a small sample’s conversion rate substantially.

The useful question is not just “Which channel has the highest percentage?” It is “What evidence would make our next decision more reliable?”
