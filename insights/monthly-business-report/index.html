BRICAL FIELD NOTES / PRACTICAL GUIDE

A useful report
ends with a next step.

Build a business report that explains the question, definitions, observations, limitations, and next action instead of presenting disconnected charts.

IN THIS GUIDE

The decision The definitions The observations The limitations The next action

By Brical Group · September 14, 2026

1. Put the decision at the top.

Before adding a chart, write down what the report is meant to help someone decide. “Review marketing” is broad. “Identify which unbooked inquiries need follow-up” is specific enough to shape the report.

A monthly report can answer several questions, but each section should earn its place. Include the reporting period, the owner, and when the underlying records were last updated.

2. Make the definitions visible.

A total is only useful when its meaning is clear. Define a lead, a booking, a completed job, or an unresolved issue in the terms your organization actually uses. Explain whether duplicates, cancellations, and incomplete records are included.

When comparing periods, check that the definition did not change. Also consider whether the comparison reflects an equivalent number of working days or a change in operating capacity.

A compact metric note: “Booked jobs: unique inquiries from this cohort with a confirmed appointment recorded by the report date. Cancellations are listed separately.”

3. Describe what the records show.

Lead with the few observations relevant to the decision. For example: “Fourteen qualified inquiries are not marked booked.” That is more actionable than presenting every available metric with equal emphasis.

Include counts alongside rates. A large percentage change based on two records should not be presented as though it carries the same weight as a stable pattern across hundreds of observations.

Use comparison periods consistently and avoid choosing a baseline solely because it makes the current result look impressive.

4. Separate the limits from the conclusions.

A report should say what it cannot establish. Missing costs prevent a defensible return-on-investment calculation. Missing outcome updates can make pipeline conversion look worse than it is. A single reporting period may not distinguish a durable pattern from ordinary variation.

Label possible explanations as hypotheses. That keeps the next conversation focused on what to check rather than defending a story the data never proved.

5. Give the next action an owner.

End with an action that someone can carry out, the person or role responsible, and a review date. “Improve follow-up” is not specific. “Review these fourteen records, update their stages, and identify the next contact action” is.

Keep a small change log. At the next review, it should be possible to tell what was changed, what remained the same, and what evidence was collected afterward.

A dashboard describes a view. A useful report supports a decision.
